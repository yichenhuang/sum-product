Below is the approach I think is most promising. It is not the “many units give high multiplicative energy” approach; that approach is tempting, but by itself it gives only lower bounds for product-set size via Cauchy–Schwarz unless one proves strong uniform orbit-size statements. The approach below instead gives a genuine containment of both \(A+A\) and \(AA\) inside comparatively small regions of a high-dimensional lattice. 1. Main idea

Take a number field \(K\) of large degree \(d\), with at least one real embedding. Embed \(K\) into its Minkowski space. Choose a finite set \(A\) of algebraic numbers whose full vector of conjugates lies inside a high-dimensional quasi-ball

\[ B_p(R)=\left\{x=(x_1,\dots,x_d)\in \mathbb R^d:\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|^p\le R^p\right\}, \]

where \(0<p\le 1\), and eventually \(p\) will be chosen very small.

Two crucial facts hold.

  1. Additive containment. Since \(0<p\le 1\),

    \[ |u+v|^p\le |u|^p+|v|^p. \]

    Hence

    \[ B_p(R)+B_p(R)\subseteq B_p(2^{1/p}R). \]

    So the sumset lives in a dilate whose volume is larger by a factor \(2^{d/p}\).

  2. Multiplicative containment. In the Minkowski embedding, multiplication of algebraic numbers becomes coordinatewise multiplication:

    \[ \sigma(ab)=(\sigma_1(a)\sigma_1(b),\dots,\sigma_d(a)\sigma_d(b)). \]

    If \(x,y\in B_p(R)\), then by Cauchy–Schwarz,

    \[ \sum_{i=1}^d |x_i y_i|^{p/2} \le \left(\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|^p\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_{i=1}^d |y_i|^p\right)^{1/2} \le R^p. \]

    Therefore

    \[ B_p(R)\cdot B_p(R)\subseteq B_{p/2}(R^2), \]

    where the product is coordinatewise.

The key point is that for small \(p\), the volume of \(B_{p/2}\) is dramatically smaller than the square of the volume of \(B_p\). Quantitatively,

\[ \left(\frac{\operatorname{vol}(B_p(1))^2}{\operatorname{vol}(B_{p/2}(1))}\right)^{1/d} \sim 2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}} \qquad (p\to 0). \]

Thus by choosing \(p\) small, the multiplicative container \(B_{p/2}(R^2)\) is smaller than the naive \(n^2\) scale by an exponential factor \(e^{\theta d}\). If the original set has size \(n\approx e^{Cd}\), then this gives

\[ |AA|\lesssim n^2 e^{-\theta d} = n^{2-\theta/C}. \]

The additive enlargement \(2^{d/p}\) is also exponential in \(d\), but by choosing \(C>\frac{\log 2}{p}\), we ensure

\[ |A+A|\lesssim 2^{d/p} n \le n^{2-\delta}. \]

So the construction reduces the theorem to finding large-degree number fields with bounded root discriminant and sufficiently regular lattice-point counting in those quasi-balls.

2. Number-field lattice setup

For clarity I first describe the totally real case. This is already enough if one uses a totally real class-field tower.

Let \(K/\mathbb Q\) be totally real of degree \(d\), with embeddings

\[ \sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_d:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb R. \]

Let

\[ \sigma:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb R^d,\qquad \sigma(\alpha)=(\sigma_1(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_d(\alpha)). \]

For a nonzero fractional ideal \(I\subset K\), the image

\[ \Lambda_I:=\sigma(I)\subset \mathbb R^d \]

is a full-rank lattice. Its covolume is

\[ \det(\Lambda_I)=N(I)\sqrt{|D_K|}, \]

where \(D_K\) is the discriminant of \(K\), and \(N(I)\) denotes the absolute norm of \(I\), extended multiplicatively to fractional ideals.

Also,

\[ \det(\Lambda_{I^2}) = N(I)^2\sqrt{|D_K|}. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{\det(\Lambda_I)^2}{\det(\Lambda_{I^2})} = \sqrt{|D_K|}. \tag{2.1} \]

This identity is important: it means the volume comparison for products is controlled only by the discriminant of \(K\), not by the particular ideal \(I\).

3. The quasi-balls \(B_p\)

For \(0<p\le 1\) and \(R>0\), define

\[ B_{p,d}(R) := \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^d: \sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|^p\le R^p \right\}. \]

When the dimension is clear, I write \(B_p(R)\).

These are not convex when \(p<1\), but they are bounded star bodies, and the subadditivity inequality \( |u+v|^p\le |u|^p+|v|^p \) is exactly what we need.

We will also need anisotropic versions. For a vector of positive radii

\[ \mathbf R=(R_1,\dots,R_d), \]

define

\[ B_p(\mathbf R) := \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^d: \sum_{i=1}^d \left|\frac{x_i}{R_i}\right|^p\le 1 \right\}. \]

Then

\[ \operatorname{vol}(B_p(\mathbf R)) = V_{p,d}\prod_{i=1}^d R_i, \]

where

\[ V_{p,d}:=\operatorname{vol}(B_p(1)). \]

The anisotropic version is useful because one can use the radii \(R_i\) to regularize the shape of the number-field lattice.

4. Volume of \(B_p(1)\)

We need the precise asymptotic of \(V_{p,d}\).

Lemma 4.1: Volume formula

For every \(p>0\),

\[ V_{p,d} = \frac{\bigl(2\Gamma(1+1/p)\bigr)^d}{\Gamma(1+d/p)}. \tag{4.1} \]

Proof

We compute the Gaussian-type integral

\[ \int_{\mathbb R^d} e^{-\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|^p}\,dx. \]

On one hand, it factors:

\[ \int_{\mathbb R^d} e^{-\sum |x_i|^p}\,dx = \left(\int_{\mathbb R} e^{-|t|^p}\,dt\right)^d. \]

Now

\[ \int_{\mathbb R} e^{-|t|^p}\,dt = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-t^p}\,dt. \]

With \(u=t^p\), so \(dt=\frac1p u^{1/p-1}\,du\), this becomes

\[ 2\cdot \frac1p \Gamma(1/p) = 2\Gamma(1+1/p). \]

Thus

\[ \int_{\mathbb R^d} e^{-\sum |x_i|^p}\,dx = \bigl(2\Gamma(1+1/p)\bigr)^d. \]

On the other hand, by radial integration for the \(p\)-homogeneous gauge,

\[ \int_{\mathbb R^d} e^{-\|x\|_p^p}\,dx = V_{p,d}\Gamma(1+d/p). \]

Equating the two expressions gives (4.1). \(\square\)

5. Additive and multiplicative containments

Lemma 5.1: Additive containment

For \(0<p\le 1\),

\[ B_p(\mathbf R)+B_p(\mathbf R) \subseteq B_p(2^{1/p}\mathbf R), \]

where

\[ 2^{1/p}\mathbf R=(2^{1/p}R_1,\dots,2^{1/p}R_d). \]

Proof

If \(x,y\in B_p(\mathbf R)\), then

\[ \sum_i \left|\frac{x_i+y_i}{R_i}\right|^p \le \sum_i \left( \left|\frac{x_i}{R_i}\right|^p + \left|\frac{y_i}{R_i}\right|^p \right) \le 2. \]

Thus

\[ \sum_i \left|\frac{x_i+y_i}{2^{1/p}R_i}\right|^p \le 1. \]

So \(x+y\in B_p(2^{1/p}\mathbf R)\). \(\square\)

Lemma 5.2: Multiplicative containment

Let coordinatewise multiplication be denoted by \(x\odot y\). Then

\[ B_p(\mathbf R)\odot B_p(\mathbf R) \subseteq B_{p/2}(\mathbf R^2), \]

where

\[ \mathbf R^2=(R_1^2,\dots,R_d^2). \]

Proof

If \(x,y\in B_p(\mathbf R)\), then

\[ \sum_i \left|\frac{x_i y_i}{R_i^2}\right|^{p/2} = \sum_i \left|\frac{x_i}{R_i}\right|^{p/2} \left|\frac{y_i}{R_i}\right|^{p/2}. \]

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \sum_i \left|\frac{x_i}{R_i}\right|^{p/2} \left|\frac{y_i}{R_i}\right|^{p/2} \le \left( \sum_i \left|\frac{x_i}{R_i}\right|^p \right)^{1/2} \left( \sum_i \left|\frac{y_i}{R_i}\right|^p \right)^{1/2} \le 1. \]

Therefore \(x\odot y\in B_{p/2}(\mathbf R^2)\). \(\square\)

6. The crucial volume ratio

Define

\[ M_{p,d} := \left(\frac{V_{p,d}^2}{V_{p/2,d}}\right)^{1/d}. \]

This is the per-coordinate volume saving in the product container.

Lemma 6.1: Limit of \(M_{p,d}\)

For fixed \(p>0\),

\[ M_{p,d} \to m_p := 2^{1+2/p} \frac{\Gamma(1+1/p)^2}{\Gamma(1+2/p)} \qquad(d\to\infty). \tag{6.1} \]

Moreover,

\[ m_p\sim 2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}} \qquad(p\to 0). \tag{6.2} \]

In particular, \(m_p\to\infty\) as \(p\to 0\).

Proof

Using (4.1),

\[ V_{p,d} = \frac{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^d}{\Gamma(1+d/p)} \]

and

\[ V_{p/2,d} = \frac{(2\Gamma(1+2/p))^d}{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}. \]

Thus

\[ \frac{V_{p,d}^2}{V_{p/2,d}} = \left( \frac{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^2}{2\Gamma(1+2/p)} \right)^d \cdot \frac{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}{\Gamma(1+d/p)^2}. \]

Taking \(d\)-th roots,

\[ M_{p,d} = \frac{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^2}{2\Gamma(1+2/p)} \left( \frac{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}{\Gamma(1+d/p)^2} \right)^{1/d}. \]

By Stirling’s formula,

\[ \left( \frac{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}{\Gamma(1+d/p)^2} \right)^{1/d} \to 2^{2/p}. \]

Therefore

\[ M_{p,d} \to 2^{2/p} \cdot \frac{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^2}{2\Gamma(1+2/p)} = 2^{1+2/p} \frac{\Gamma(1+1/p)^2}{\Gamma(1+2/p)}. \]

This proves (6.1).

For (6.2), put \(a=1/p\). As \(a\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{\Gamma(1+a)^2}{\Gamma(1+2a)} \sim \sqrt{\pi a}\,2^{-2a}. \]

Therefore

\[ m_p = 2^{1+2a} \frac{\Gamma(1+a)^2}{\Gamma(1+2a)} \sim 2^{1+2a} \cdot \sqrt{\pi a}\,2^{-2a} = 2\sqrt{\pi a} = 2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}. \]

So \(m_p\to\infty\) as \(p\to 0\). \(\square\)

7. Conditional construction theorem

The following proposition is the core reduction. It isolates exactly the number-field/lattice-counting input that remains.

Proposition 7.1: Conditional sum-product construction

Let \(K_j\) be a sequence of totally real number fields with degrees

\[ d_j=[K_j:\mathbb Q]\to\infty. \]

Write

\[ \Delta_j:=\sqrt{|D_{K_j}|}. \]

Assume that there is a constant \(M<\infty\) such that

\[ \Delta_j^{1/d_j}\le M \qquad\text{for all }j. \tag{7.1} \]

Fix \(0<p\le 1\), and suppose

\[ m_p>M, \tag{7.2} \]

where \(m_p\) is the constant from Lemma 6.1.

Assume also the following regular lattice-counting hypothesis.

There exist fractional ideals \(I_j\subset K_j\), anisotropic radii

\[ \mathbf R_j=(R_{j,1},\dots,R_{j,d_j}), \]

and a constant \(C>0\) such that

\[ C>\frac{\log 2}{p}, \tag{7.3} \]

and, writing

\[ \Lambda_j=\sigma(I_j), \]
\[ P_j:=I_j\cap B_p(\mathbf R_j), \]

we have

\[ |P_j| = e^{(C+o(1))d_j}. \tag{7.4} \]

Moreover, the following volume-regular upper bounds hold:

\[ |I_j\cap B_p(2^{1/p}\mathbf R_j)| \le e^{o(d_j)} \cdot 2^{d_j/p} |P_j|, \tag{7.5} \]

and

\[ |I_j^2\cap B_{p/2}(\mathbf R_j^2)| \le e^{o(d_j)} \cdot |P_j|^2 \left(\frac{\Delta_j^{1/d_j}}{M_{p,d_j}}\right)^{d_j}. \tag{7.6} \]

Then there exists \(\delta>0\) and an infinite sequence of finite sets \(A_j\subset\mathbb R\), \(|A_j|\to\infty\), such that

\[ \max(|A_j+A_j|,|A_jA_j|) \le |A_j|^{2-\delta} \]

for all sufficiently large \(j\).

Proof

Fix a real embedding \(\sigma_1:K_j\hookrightarrow\mathbb R\). Define

\[ A_j:=\sigma_1(P_j)\subset\mathbb R. \]

Because \(\sigma_1\) is injective,

\[ |A_j|=|P_j|. \]

Let

\[ n_j:=|A_j|=|P_j|. \]

By (7.4),

\[ n_j=e^{(C+o(1))d_j}. \tag{7.7} \]

Sumset bound

By Lemma 5.1,

\[ P_j+P_j \subseteq I_j\cap B_p(2^{1/p}\mathbf R_j). \]

Therefore, using (7.5),

\[ |A_j+A_j| = |P_j+P_j| \le e^{o(d_j)}2^{d_j/p}n_j. \]

Taking logs and using (7.7),

\[ \log |A_j+A_j| \le \log n_j+\frac{\log 2}{p}d_j+o(d_j). \]

Since \(\log n_j=(C+o(1))d_j\),

\[ \frac{\log |A_j+A_j|}{\log n_j} \le 1+\frac{\log 2}{pC}+o(1). \]

Because \(C>\frac{\log 2}{p}\), the number

\[ \delta_+:= 1-\frac{\log 2}{pC} \]

is positive. Hence for any

\[ 0<\delta<\delta_+, \]

we have, for all sufficiently large \(j\),

\[ |A_j+A_j| \le n_j^{2-\delta}. \tag{7.8} \]

Product-set bound

By Lemma 5.2,

\[ P_jP_j \subseteq I_j^2\cap B_{p/2}(\mathbf R_j^2). \]

Therefore, by (7.6),

\[ |A_jA_j| = |P_jP_j| \le e^{o(d_j)} n_j^2 \left(\frac{\Delta_j^{1/d_j}}{M_{p,d_j}}\right)^{d_j}. \]

By (7.1),

\[ \Delta_j^{1/d_j}\le M. \]

By Lemma 6.1,

\[ M_{p,d_j}\to m_p. \]

Since \(m_p>M\), choose

\[ \theta:=\log\left(\frac{m_p}{M}\right)>0. \]

Then for all large \(j\),

\[ \log\left(\frac{M_{p,d_j}}{\Delta_j^{1/d_j}}\right) \ge \frac{\theta}{2}. \]

Hence

\[ |A_jA_j| \le e^{o(d_j)} n_j^2 e^{-\theta d_j/2}. \]

Using \(\log n_j=(C+o(1))d_j\), this implies

\[ \frac{\log |A_jA_j|}{\log n_j} \le 2-\frac{\theta}{2C}+o(1). \]

Thus for any

\[ 0<\delta<\frac{\theta}{2C}, \]

we have, for all sufficiently large \(j\),

\[ |A_jA_j| \le n_j^{2-\delta}. \tag{7.9} \]

Combining (7.8) and (7.9), any

\[ 0<\delta< \min\left( 1-\frac{\log 2}{pC}, \frac{1}{2C}\log\frac{m_p}{M} \right) \tag{7.10} \]

works for all sufficiently large \(j\).

Since \(d_j\to\infty\), (7.7) gives \(n_j\to\infty\). This proves the proposition. \(\square\)

8. Why the parameter choice is possible

The only inequalities we need are

\[ m_p>M \]

and

\[ C>\frac{\log 2}{p}. \]

The first is possible because \(m_p\to\infty\) as \(p\to 0\). Once \(p\) is chosen, choose any sufficiently large constant \(C\), for example

\[ C=2\frac{\log 2}{p}. \]

Then

\[ 1-\frac{\log 2}{pC} = 1-\frac12 = \frac12, \]

so the sumset side has a healthy margin.

The product-side saving is

\[ \frac{1}{2C}\log\frac{m_p}{M}. \]

For very small \(p\), this may be tiny, but it is positive. Therefore the resulting \(\delta\) may be extremely small, but it is an absolute positive constant once the tower and \(p,C\) are fixed.

9. Source of bounded root discriminant fields

The number-field input needed for (7.1) is standard.

Martinet-type class-field-tower input

There exists an infinite sequence of totally real number fields

\[ K_1\subset K_2\subset K_3\subset\cdots \]

with

\[ [K_j:\mathbb Q]\to\infty \]

and uniformly bounded root discriminant

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_j):=|D_{K_j}|^{1/[K_j:\mathbb Q]} \le B_0 \]

for some absolute constant \(B_0\).

Equivalently,

\[ \Delta_j^{1/d_j} = |D_{K_j}|^{1/(2d_j)} = \operatorname{rd}(K_j)^{1/2} \le B_0^{1/2}. \]

Thus in Proposition 7.1 one may take

\[ M=B_0^{1/2}. \]

One standard source is a totally real field with an infinite narrow Hilbert class-field tower. In an unramified tower, the root discriminant remains constant.

So bounded root discriminant itself is not the main obstruction.

10. The main remaining gap

The real remaining gap is the regular lattice-counting hypothesis in Proposition 7.1.

Let me state it cleanly.

Gap G: Regular adelic lattice-counting lemma

Let \(K_j\) be a totally real bounded-root-discriminant tower, \(d_j\to\infty\). Fix \(0<p\le 1\) and \(C>\frac{\log 2}{p}\).

One needs to prove that for each \(j\), there exist:

  • a fractional ideal \(I_j\subset K_j\);
  • positive radii \(\mathbf R_j=(R_{j,1},\dots,R_{j,d_j})\);

such that, with

\[ P_j=I_j\cap B_p(\mathbf R_j), \]

the following hold:

  1. Correct size:

    \[ |P_j|=e^{(C+o(1))d_j}. \]

  2. Additive upper regularity:

    \[ |I_j\cap B_p(2^{1/p}\mathbf R_j)| \le e^{o(d_j)}2^{d_j/p}|P_j|. \]

  3. Multiplicative upper regularity:

    \[ |I_j^2\cap B_{p/2}(\mathbf R_j^2)| \le e^{o(d_j)} |P_j|^2 \left(\frac{\Delta_j^{1/d_j}}{M_{p,d_j}}\right)^{d_j}. \]

Equivalently, the lattice counts should be volume-regular at the relevant scales:

\[ |I_j\cap B_p(\mathbf R_j)| \approx \frac{V_{p,d_j}\prod_i R_{j,i}}{\det(\sigma(I_j))} \]

and

\[ |I_j^2\cap B_{p/2}(\mathbf R_j^2)| \lesssim \frac{V_{p/2,d_j}\prod_i R_{j,i}^2}{\det(\sigma(I_j^2))} \]

up to \(e^{o(d_j)}\) factors.

11. Why Gap G is plausible

There are several reasons this gap looks plausible rather than structurally impossible.

11.1. Fractional ideals give flexibility

If we used only \(I_j=\mathcal O_{K_j}\) and only isotropic radii \(R_1=\cdots=R_d\), the Minkowski lattice might be skew, and lattice-point counts in \(B_p\) might be irregular.

Allowing fractional ideals and anisotropic radii is equivalent to allowing an Arakelov divisor. This gives substantial freedom to regularize the lattice.

The product comparison survives this freedom because

\[ \frac{\det(\sigma(I_j))^2}{\det(\sigma(I_j^2))} = \sqrt{|D_{K_j}|}. \]

So the main product-volume saving is unaffected by the ideal choice.

11.2. The required accuracy is only \(e^{o(d)}\)

We do not need sharp asymptotics with \(1+o(1)\) error. We only need counts correct up to \(e^{o(d)}\) factors.

That is much weaker than classical lattice-point asymptotics in high dimension.

11.3. Bounded root discriminant gives bounded covolume per dimension

The lattice covolume satisfies

\[ \det(\sigma(I_j))^{1/d_j} = N(I_j)^{1/d_j}\Delta_j^{1/d_j}. \]

The field contribution \(\Delta_j^{1/d_j}\) is bounded. Thus after choosing \(I_j\) and \(\mathbf R_j\) appropriately, the natural volume scale is exponential \(e^{Cd_j}\), exactly the regime needed.

11.4. Possible methods to prove Gap G

There are several plausible routes.

Route A: Arakelov semistability

A choice of fractional ideal and archimedean radii is an Arakelov lattice. One can try to choose the Arakelov divisor so that the associated lattice is semistable or well-rounded. For semistable lattices, high-dimensional volume estimates often imply lattice-point upper bounds in star bodies up to subexponential factors.

The simultaneous appearance of \(I_j\) and \(I_j^2\) means one needs semistability/control for both the lattice and its square. That is nontrivial but still plausible.

Route B: Averaging over the Arakelov torus

The group of archimedean scalings modulo the unit lattice gives a compact Arakelov torus. One may average the relevant counting functions over this torus. If the mean count is controlled by the expected volume, then Markov’s inequality can produce a scaling for which the upper bounds hold simultaneously.

One also needs a lower bound for the count in \(B_p(\mathbf R_j)\). That might be obtained either by a second-moment argument or by selecting the scale slightly above the volume threshold.

Route C: Use a Galois tower with symmetric embeddings

If the bounded-root-discriminant tower can be chosen Galois over \(\mathbb Q\), then the Minkowski lattices have large symmetry groups permuting the embeddings. This symmetry may force enough regularity for isotropic \(B_p(R)\) balls, avoiding the need for anisotropic radii.

This is attractive but may depend on the specific tower.

12. Seriousness of the gap

Gap G is the serious remaining obstruction in this approach.

It cannot be ignored, because arbitrary lattices of determinant \(e^{O(d)}\) can be extremely skew. For a highly skew lattice, the number of lattice points in \(B_p(\mathbf R)\) or \(B_{p/2}(\mathbf R^2)\) can differ from volume predictions by exponential factors.

The hope is that number-field ideal lattices in bounded-root-discriminant towers, after Arakelov regularization, are not arbitrary lattices and can be made sufficiently regular.

So Gap G is plausibly closeable, but it is the part that needs real work.

13. Why I do not recommend the unit-energy approach as the main route

A natural idea is to use many units \(u\in\mathcal O_K^\times\) so that multiplication by \(u\) maps much of \(A\) back into \(A\). Then for many pairs \((a,b)\),

\[ ab=(ua)(u^{-1}b), \]

giving many multiplicative collisions.

This gives large multiplicative energy. However, large energy alone does not upper-bound \(|AA|\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[ E_\times(A)\ge \frac{|A|^4}{|AA|}, \]

or equivalently

\[ |AA|\ge \frac{|A|^4}{E_\times(A)}. \]

That is a lower bound for \(|AA|\), not an upper bound. To turn unit symmetries into an upper bound, one would need to show that almost every product has many representations, i.e. that the unit action gives large orbits uniformly across most of \(A\times A\). That is a much stronger and more delicate statement.

The quasi-norm/volume-containment approach avoids this issue entirely because it gives a direct inclusion

\[ AA\subseteq I^2\cap B_{p/2}(\mathbf R^2), \]

and therefore a direct upper bound.

14. Summary of the proof plan

Here is the complete step-by-step plan.

Step 1: Choose a bounded-root-discriminant totally real tower

Use a Martinet-type infinite narrow class-field tower

\[ K_1\subset K_2\subset\cdots \]

with

\[ d_j=[K_j:\mathbb Q]\to\infty \]

and

\[ \Delta_j^{1/d_j}\le M \]

for some absolute \(M<\infty\).

Step 2: Choose \(p\)

Since

\[ m_p\sim 2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}, \]

choose \(p>0\) sufficiently small that

\[ m_p>M. \]

Let

\[ \theta:=\log\frac{m_p}{M}>0. \]

Step 3: Choose \(C\)

Choose

\[ C>\frac{\log 2}{p}. \]

For example,

\[ C=2\frac{\log 2}{p}. \]

Step 4: Prove the regular lattice-counting lemma

For each \(K_j\), find a fractional ideal \(I_j\) and radii \(\mathbf R_j\) such that

\[ P_j:=I_j\cap B_p(\mathbf R_j) \]

satisfies

\[ |P_j|=e^{(C+o(1))d_j}, \]

and the additive/multiplicative container counts satisfy the regular estimates of Gap G.

This is the main remaining technical step.

Step 5: Define the real set

Choose one real embedding \(\sigma_1:K_j\hookrightarrow\mathbb R\), and set

\[ A_j:=\sigma_1(P_j)\subset\mathbb R. \]

Then

\[ |A_j|=|P_j|=e^{(C+o(1))d_j}\to\infty. \]

Step 6: Bound the sumset

By subadditivity,

\[ P_j+P_j\subseteq I_j\cap B_p(2^{1/p}\mathbf R_j). \]

Therefore

\[ |A_j+A_j| \le e^{o(d_j)} 2^{d_j/p}|A_j|. \]

Since \(|A_j|=e^{(C+o(1))d_j}\),

\[ |A_j+A_j| \le |A_j|^{1+\frac{\log 2}{pC}+o(1)}. \]

Because \(C>\frac{\log 2}{p}\), this is at most

\[ |A_j|^{2-\delta} \]

for some fixed \(\delta>0\).

Step 7: Bound the product set

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ P_jP_j\subseteq I_j^2\cap B_{p/2}(\mathbf R_j^2). \]

The regular counting estimate gives

\[ |A_jA_j| \le e^{o(d_j)} |A_j|^2 \left(\frac{\Delta_j^{1/d_j}}{M_{p,d_j}}\right)^{d_j}. \]

Since

\[ \Delta_j^{1/d_j}\le M<m_p \]

and \(M_{p,d_j}\to m_p\), we get

\[ |A_jA_j| \le |A_j|^2 e^{-\theta d_j/2} \]

for large \(j\). Since \(|A_j|=e^{(C+o(1))d_j}\),

\[ |A_jA_j| \le |A_j|^{2-\theta/(3C)} \]

for large \(j\), after adjusting constants.

One may take any

\[ 0<\delta< \min\left( 1-\frac{\log 2}{pC}, \frac{1}{2C}\log\frac{m_p}{M} \right), \]

with small margins to absorb the \(o(1)\)-terms.

Then

\[ \max(|A_j+A_j|,|A_jA_j|) \le |A_j|^{2-\delta} \]

for all sufficiently large \(j\).

Complete proof

We prove the theorem by constructing \(A\) from algebraic integers in number fields of large degree and bounded root discriminant.

The key construction is:

\[ A=\sigma_1\left(\mathcal O_K\cap B_{p,d}(R)\right)\subset \mathbb R, \]

where

  • \(K/\mathbb Q\) is a totally real number field of degree \(d\);
  • \(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_d:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb R\) are its real embeddings;
  • \(\sigma=(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_d)\) is the Minkowski embedding;
  • \(B_{p,d}(R)\subset\mathbb R^d\) is the \(\ell^p\)-quasi-ball
\[ B_{p,d}(R)=\left\{x=(x_1,\dots,x_d):\sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^p\le R^p\right\}; \]
  • \(0<p\le 1\) is chosen small;
  • \(R\) is chosen so that \(\mathcal O_K\cap B_{p,d}(R)\) has size about \(e^{Cd}\), with \(C\) a large fixed constant.

The reason this works is:

  1. \(B_p(R)+B_p(R)\subseteq B_p(2^{1/p}R)\), so the sumset is controlled by a modest enlargement of the original body.

  2. Multiplication in the Minkowski embedding is coordinatewise:

    \[ \sigma(\alpha\beta)=\sigma(\alpha)\odot \sigma(\beta), \]

    and Cauchy–Schwarz gives

    \[ B_p(R)\odot B_p(R)\subseteq B_{p/2}(R^2). \]
  3. For small \(p\), the volume of \(B_{p/2,d}(R^2)\) is smaller than the square of the volume of \(B_{p,d}(R)\) by an exponential factor in \(d\).
  4. Bounded root discriminant lets us count algebraic integers in these bodies accurately enough.

The counting point is the main ingredient. In the previous plan this was left as a gap. Here it is closed using a covering-radius estimate coming from the codifferent and a standard transference theorem.

1. Number-field input: bounded root discriminant towers

We use the following classical theorem of Martinet.

Theorem 1.1: Martinet tower

There exists an infinite sequence of totally real number fields

\[ K_i/\mathbb Q \]

with

\[ d_i=[K_i:\mathbb Q]\to\infty \]

and uniformly bounded root discriminant:

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i):=|D_{K_i}|^{1/d_i}\le R_0 \]

for some absolute constant \(R_0<\infty\).

Equivalently, if

\[ \Delta_i:=|D_{K_i}|^{1/2}, \]

then

\[ \Delta_i^{1/d_i}\le M, \qquad M:=R_0^{1/2}. \tag{1.1} \]

This follows from Martinet’s construction of an infinite totally real class-field tower. In an unramified tower, the root discriminant remains constant.

2. Volumes of \(\ell^p\)-quasi-balls

For \(s>0\), define

\[ B_{s,d}(R) = \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^d: \sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^s\le R^s \right\}. \]

Let

\[ V_{s,d}:=\operatorname{vol}(B_{s,d}(1)). \]

Lemma 2.1: Volume formula

For every \(s>0\),

\[ V_{s,d} = \frac{\left(2\Gamma(1+1/s)\right)^d}{\Gamma(1+d/s)}. \tag{2.1} \]

Proof

Compute

\[ I:=\int_{\mathbb R^d} e^{-\sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^s}\,dx. \]

On one hand, the integral factors:

\[ I= \left( \int_{\mathbb R} e^{-|t|^s}\,dt \right)^d. \]

Now

\[ \int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|t|^s}\,dt = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-t^s}\,dt. \]

With \(u=t^s\), we get

\[ 2\int_0^\infty e^{-t^s}\,dt = 2\cdot \frac1s \Gamma(1/s) = 2\Gamma(1+1/s). \]

Hence

\[ I=\left(2\Gamma(1+1/s)\right)^d. \]

On the other hand, using radial integration for the \(s\)-homogeneous gauge,

\[ I=V_{s,d}\Gamma(1+d/s). \]

Equating the two expressions gives (2.1). \(\square\)

3. Additive and multiplicative containments

We use the following elementary inequalities.

Lemma 3.1: Additive containment

For \(0<p\le1\),

\[ B_{p,d}(R)+B_{p,d}(R) \subseteq B_{p,d}(2^{1/p}R). \tag{3.1} \]

Proof

For real numbers \(u,v\) and \(0<p\le1\),

\[ |u+v|^p\le |u|^p+|v|^p. \]

Thus, if \(x,y\in B_{p,d}(R)\), then

\[ \sum_{j=1}^d |x_j+y_j|^p \le \sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^p+\sum_{j=1}^d |y_j|^p \le 2R^p. \]

Equivalently,

\[ x+y\in B_{p,d}(2^{1/p}R). \]

\(\square\)

Lemma 3.2: Multiplicative containment

Let \(\odot\) denote coordinatewise multiplication. For \(0<p\le1\),

\[ B_{p,d}(R)\odot B_{p,d}(R) \subseteq B_{p/2,d}(R^2). \tag{3.2} \]

Proof

Let \(q=p/2\). If \(x,y\in B_{p,d}(R)\), then

\[ \sum_{j=1}^d |x_jy_j|^q = \sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^{p/2}|y_j|^{p/2}. \]

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^{p/2}|y_j|^{p/2} \le \left(\sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^p\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_{j=1}^d |y_j|^p\right)^{1/2} \le R^p. \]

Since \(q=p/2\), we have

\[ (R^2)^q=R^p. \]

Therefore

\[ x\odot y\in B_{q,d}(R^2)=B_{p/2,d}(R^2). \]

\(\square\)

4. The product-volume saving

Define

\[ M_{p,d} := \left(\frac{V_{p,d}^2}{V_{p/2,d}}\right)^{1/d}. \]

This is the per-dimension volume saving in passing from two \(p\)-balls to one \(p/2\)-ball.

Lemma 4.1

For fixed \(p>0\),

\[ M_{p,d}\to m_p \]

as \(d\to\infty\), where

\[ m_p = 2^{1+2/p} \frac{\Gamma(1+1/p)^2}{\Gamma(1+2/p)}. \tag{4.1} \]

Moreover,

\[ m_p\sim 2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}} \qquad(p\to0). \tag{4.2} \]

In particular,

\[ m_p\to\infty \qquad(p\to0). \]

Proof

By Lemma 2.1,

\[ V_{p,d} = \frac{\left(2\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^d}{\Gamma(1+d/p)} \]

and

\[ V_{p/2,d} = \frac{\left(2\Gamma(1+2/p)\right)^d}{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{V_{p,d}^2}{V_{p/2,d}} = \left( \frac{\left(2\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^2}{2\Gamma(1+2/p)} \right)^d \cdot \frac{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}{\Gamma(1+d/p)^2}. \]

Taking \(d\)-th roots,

\[ M_{p,d} = \frac{\left(2\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^2}{2\Gamma(1+2/p)} \left( \frac{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}{\Gamma(1+d/p)^2} \right)^{1/d}. \]

By Stirling’s formula,

\[ \left( \frac{\Gamma(1+2d/p)}{\Gamma(1+d/p)^2} \right)^{1/d} \to 2^{2/p}. \]

Thus

\[ M_{p,d} \to 2^{2/p} \cdot \frac{\left(2\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^2}{2\Gamma(1+2/p)} = 2^{1+2/p} \frac{\Gamma(1+1/p)^2}{\Gamma(1+2/p)}. \]

This proves (4.1).

For (4.2), put \(a=1/p\). Then as \(a\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{\Gamma(1+a)^2}{\Gamma(1+2a)} \sim \sqrt{\pi a}\,2^{-2a}. \]

Hence

\[ m_p = 2^{1+2a} \frac{\Gamma(1+a)^2}{\Gamma(1+2a)} \sim 2^{1+2a}\sqrt{\pi a}\,2^{-2a} = 2\sqrt{\pi a} = 2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}. \]

So \(m_p\to\infty\) as \(p\to0\). \(\square\)

5. Counting algebraic integers in \(B_{s,d}(R)\)

Let \(K/\mathbb Q\) be totally real of degree \(d\). Let

\[ \sigma:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb R^d \]

be the Minkowski embedding

\[ \sigma(\alpha)=(\sigma_1(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_d(\alpha)). \]

Let

\[ \Lambda_K:=\sigma(\mathcal O_K). \]

Then \(\Lambda_K\) is a full-rank lattice in \(\mathbb R^d\) with covolume

\[ \det(\Lambda_K)=\Delta_K:=|D_K|^{1/2}. \tag{5.1} \]

We need a uniform counting estimate for

\[ N_{K,s}(R) := |\mathcal O_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)| = |\Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)|. \]

The following lemma is the key point that closes the earlier gap.

Lemma 5.1: Covering-radius counting estimate

Let \(K/\mathbb Q\) be totally real of degree \(d\), and suppose

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K)=|D_K|^{1/d}\le R_0. \]

Fix \(0<s\le1\). Put

\[ \mu_s:=\frac{R_0}{2}d^{1/s}. \]

Then for every \(R>\mu_s\),

\[ \frac{V_{s,d}R^d}{\Delta_K} \left(1-\left(\frac{\mu_s}{R}\right)^s\right)^{d/s} \le N_{K,s}(R) \le \frac{V_{s,d}R^d}{\Delta_K} \left(1+\left(\frac{\mu_s}{R}\right)^s\right)^{d/s}. \tag{5.2} \]

Proof

We first bound the Euclidean covering radius of \(\Lambda_K\).

Let \(\Lambda_K^*\) denote the Euclidean dual lattice. For totally real \(K\),

\[ \Lambda_K^*=\sigma(\mathfrak d_K^{-1}), \]

where \(\mathfrak d_K^{-1}\) is the codifferent.

Let \(\beta\in\mathfrak d_K^{-1}\), \(\beta\neq0\). Then

\[ \beta\mathfrak d_K\subseteq\mathcal O_K. \]

Therefore the fractional ideal \((\beta)\mathfrak d_K\) is an integral ideal, so

\[ N((\beta)\mathfrak d_K) = |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)|\,N(\mathfrak d_K) = |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)|\,|D_K| \ge 1. \]

Thus

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)| \ge |D_K|^{-1}. \]

By AM–GM,

\[ \|\sigma(\beta)\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^d |\sigma_j(\beta)|^2 \ge d \left( \prod_{j=1}^d |\sigma_j(\beta)|^2 \right)^{1/d} = d\,|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)|^{2/d}. \]

Hence

\[ \|\sigma(\beta)\|_2 \ge \sqrt d\,|D_K|^{-1/d}. \]

Since \(\operatorname{rd}(K)=|D_K|^{1/d}\le R_0\), we get

\[ \lambda_1(\Lambda_K^*) \ge \frac{\sqrt d}{R_0}. \tag{5.3} \]

Now use the standard transference bound for the Euclidean covering radius \(\rho_2(\Lambda_K)\):

\[ \rho_2(\Lambda_K)\lambda_1(\Lambda_K^*)\le \frac d2. \]

Combining this with (5.3),

\[ \rho_2(\Lambda_K) \le \frac{R_0}{2}\sqrt d. \tag{5.4} \]

For \(0<s\le1\), we have

\[ \|x\|_s \le d^{1/s-1/2}\|x\|_2. \]

Therefore the \(s\)-quasinorm covering radius satisfies

\[ \rho_s(\Lambda_K) \le d^{1/s-1/2}\rho_2(\Lambda_K) \le \frac{R_0}{2}d^{1/s} = \mu_s. \tag{5.5} \]

Let \(F\) be a measurable fundamental domain for \(\Lambda_K\) contained in \(B_{s,d}(\mu_s)\). For example, one may take a Euclidean Voronoi cell and use (5.5).

We now compare volumes.

Because \(0<s\le1\),

\[ |u+v|^s\le |u|^s+|v|^s. \tag{5.6} \]

Thus, if \(\lambda\in\Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)\) and \(f\in F\subseteq B_{s,d}(\mu_s)\), then

\[ \lambda+f\in B_{s,d}\left((R^s+\mu_s^s)^{1/s}\right). \]

The translates \(\lambda+F\) are disjoint up to boundaries, and each has volume \(\Delta_K\). Therefore

\[ N_{K,s}(R)\Delta_K \le \operatorname{vol} B_{s,d}\left((R^s+\mu_s^s)^{1/s}\right). \]

Using homogeneity of volume,

\[ \operatorname{vol} B_{s,d}\left((R^s+\mu_s^s)^{1/s}\right) = V_{s,d}R^d \left(1+\left(\frac{\mu_s}{R}\right)^s\right)^{d/s}. \]

This gives the upper bound in (5.2).

For the lower bound, let

\[ R_-:=(R^s-\mu_s^s)^{1/s}. \]

Every point \(z\in B_{s,d}(R_-)\) lies in some translate \(\lambda+F\). Write \(z=\lambda+f\), with \(f\in F\). Then

\[ \lambda=z-f. \]

By (5.6),

\[ \|\lambda\|_s^s \le \|z\|_s^s+\|f\|_s^s \le (R^s-\mu_s^s)+\mu_s^s = R^s. \]

So \(\lambda\in\Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)\). Hence

\[ B_{s,d}(R_-) \subseteq \bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)}(\lambda+F). \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{vol} B_{s,d}(R_-) \le N_{K,s}(R)\Delta_K. \]

Again by homogeneity,

\[ \operatorname{vol} B_{s,d}(R_-) = V_{s,d}R^d \left(1-\left(\frac{\mu_s}{R}\right)^s\right)^{d/s}. \]

This proves the lower bound in (5.2). \(\square\)

6. Parameter selection

Let \(K_i\) be the Martinet tower from Theorem 1.1. Write

\[ d_i=[K_i:\mathbb Q], \qquad \Delta_i=|D_{K_i}|^{1/2}. \]

Recall that

\[ \Delta_i^{1/d_i}\le M:=R_0^{1/2}. \tag{6.1} \]

Since \(m_p\to\infty\) as \(p\to0\), choose \(0<p\le1\) such that

\[ m_p>M. \]

Define

\[ \theta_0:=\log\frac{m_p}{M}>0. \]

By Lemma 4.1,

\[ M_{p,d}\to m_p. \]

Hence, after discarding finitely many \(i\), we may assume that

\[ \frac{M_{p,d_i}}{\Delta_i^{1/d_i}} \ge e^\theta, \qquad \theta:=\frac{\theta_0}{2}>0. \tag{6.2} \]

Equivalently,

\[ \left(\frac{\Delta_i^{1/d_i}}{M_{p,d_i}}\right)^{d_i} \le e^{-\theta d_i}. \tag{6.3} \]

Now put

\[ L:=\frac{\log 2}{p}. \]

We will choose a large constant \(C>L\). The largeness of \(C\) is used only to make the lattice-counting errors negligible.

7. Choosing the radius \(R_i\)

For each \(i\), define \(R_i>0\) by

\[ \frac{V_{p,d_i}R_i^{d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{Cd_i}. \tag{7.1} \]

Equivalently,

\[ R_i = \left(\frac{e^{Cd_i}\Delta_i}{V_{p,d_i}}\right)^{1/d_i}. \]

We will use Lemma 5.1 to estimate the number of algebraic integers in \(B_{p,d_i}(R_i)\), in \(B_{p,d_i}(2^{1/p}R_i)\), and in \(B_{p/2,d_i}(R_i^2)\).

Let

\[ q:=p/2. \]

By Lemma 2.1 and Stirling’s formula,

\[ d^{1/p}V_{p,d}^{1/d} \]

stays bounded above and below by positive constants depending only on \(p\). Thus, from (7.1),

\[ R_i\asymp_{p,R_0} e^C d_i^{1/p}. \tag{7.2} \]

Similarly,

\[ R_i^2\asymp_{p,R_0} e^{2C}d_i^{2/p} = e^{2C}d_i^{1/q}. \tag{7.3} \]

On the other hand, Lemma 5.1 gives covering radii

\[ \mu_p\ll_{R_0} d_i^{1/p}, \qquad \mu_q\ll_{R_0} d_i^{1/q}. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{\mu_p}{R_i} \ll_{p,R_0} e^{-C}, \tag{7.4} \]

and

\[ \frac{\mu_q}{R_i^2} \ll_{p,R_0} e^{-2C}. \tag{7.5} \]

Consequently, by Lemma 5.1, there is a function

\[ \varepsilon(C)=O_{p,R_0}(e^{-pC}) \]

with

\[ \varepsilon(C)\to0 \qquad(C\to\infty), \tag{7.6} \]

such that for all sufficiently large \(i\),

\[ e^{-\varepsilon(C)d_i} \frac{V_{p,d_i}R_i^{d_i}}{\Delta_i} \le N_{K_i,p}(R_i) \le e^{\varepsilon(C)d_i} \frac{V_{p,d_i}R_i^{d_i}}{\Delta_i}, \tag{7.7} \]
\[ N_{K_i,p}(2^{1/p}R_i) \le e^{\varepsilon(C)d_i} \frac{V_{p,d_i}(2^{1/p}R_i)^{d_i}}{\Delta_i}, \tag{7.8} \]

and

\[ N_{K_i,q}(R_i^2) \le e^{\varepsilon(C)d_i} \frac{V_{q,d_i}R_i^{2d_i}}{\Delta_i}. \tag{7.9} \]

Choose \(C>L\) so large that, writing

\[ \varepsilon:=\varepsilon(C), \]

we have

\[ \varepsilon<\frac{C-L}{4} \tag{7.10} \]

and

\[ \varepsilon<\frac{\theta}{4}. \tag{7.11} \]

This is possible by (7.6).

8. Constructing the sets

For each sufficiently large \(i\), define

\[ P_i := \left\{ \alpha\in\mathcal O_{K_i}: \sigma(\alpha)\in B_{p,d_i}(R_i) \right\}. \]

Choose one real embedding

\[ \sigma_{i,1}:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb R. \]

Set

\[ A_i:=\sigma_{i,1}(P_i)\subset\mathbb R. \]

Since \(\sigma_{i,1}\) is injective,

\[ |A_i|=|P_i|. \]

Let

\[ n_i:=|A_i|. \]

By (7.1) and (7.7),

\[ e^{(C-\varepsilon)d_i} \le n_i \le e^{(C+\varepsilon)d_i}. \tag{8.1} \]

In particular,

\[ n_i\to\infty \]

because \(d_i\to\infty\).

9. Sumset estimate

We have

\[ A_i+A_i = \sigma_{i,1}(P_i+P_i). \]

Because \(\sigma_{i,1}\) is injective,

\[ |A_i+A_i|=|P_i+P_i|. \]

By Lemma 3.1,

\[ P_i+P_i \subseteq \mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{p,d_i}(2^{1/p}R_i). \]

Therefore, by (7.8),

\[ |A_i+A_i| \le N_{K_i,p}(2^{1/p}R_i) \le e^{\varepsilon d_i} \frac{V_{p,d_i}(2^{1/p}R_i)^{d_i}}{\Delta_i}. \]

Using (7.1),

\[ \frac{V_{p,d_i}R_i^{d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{Cd_i}. \]

Hence

\[ |A_i+A_i| \le e^{\varepsilon d_i} 2^{d_i/p} e^{Cd_i} = e^{(C+L+\varepsilon)d_i}, \tag{9.1} \]

where \(L=\frac{\log2}{p}\).

Using the lower bound in (8.1),

\[ d_i\le \frac{\log n_i}{C-\varepsilon}. \]

Thus

\[ \log |A_i+A_i| \le \frac{C+L+\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon} \log n_i. \]

Now

\[ 2-\frac{C+L+\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon} = \frac{C-L-3\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon}. \]

By (7.10), this is positive. Define

\[ \delta_+ := \frac{C-L-3\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon} >0. \tag{9.2} \]

Then

\[ |A_i+A_i| \le n_i^{2-\delta_+}. \tag{9.3} \]

10. Product-set estimate

Similarly,

\[ A_iA_i = \sigma_{i,1}(P_iP_i), \]

and therefore

\[ |A_iA_i|=|P_iP_i|. \]

By Lemma 3.2,

\[ P_iP_i \subseteq \mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{q,d_i}(R_i^2), \qquad q=p/2. \]

Thus, using (7.9),

\[ |A_iA_i| \le N_{K_i,q}(R_i^2) \le e^{\varepsilon d_i} \frac{V_{q,d_i}R_i^{2d_i}}{\Delta_i}. \tag{10.1} \]

We now rewrite the volume term in terms of \(n_i\).

From (7.1),

\[ R_i^{d_i} = \frac{e^{Cd_i}\Delta_i}{V_{p,d_i}}. \]

Hence

\[ R_i^{2d_i} = \frac{e^{2Cd_i}\Delta_i^2}{V_{p,d_i}^2}. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{V_{q,d_i}R_i^{2d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{2Cd_i} \Delta_i \frac{V_{q,d_i}}{V_{p,d_i}^2}. \]

Since

\[ M_{p,d_i}^{d_i} = \frac{V_{p,d_i}^2}{V_{q,d_i}}, \]

we get

\[ \frac{V_{q,d_i}R_i^{2d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{2Cd_i} \left( \frac{\Delta_i^{1/d_i}}{M_{p,d_i}} \right)^{d_i}. \]

Using (6.3),

\[ \left( \frac{\Delta_i^{1/d_i}}{M_{p,d_i}} \right)^{d_i} \le e^{-\theta d_i}. \]

Therefore (10.1) gives

\[ |A_iA_i| \le e^{(2C-\theta+\varepsilon)d_i}. \tag{10.2} \]

Again using \(d_i\le \frac{\log n_i}{C-\varepsilon}\),

\[ \log |A_iA_i| \le \frac{2C-\theta+\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon} \log n_i. \]

Now

\[ 2-\frac{2C-\theta+\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon} = \frac{\theta-3\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon}. \]

By (7.11), this is positive. Define

\[ \delta_\times := \frac{\theta-3\varepsilon}{C-\varepsilon} >0. \tag{10.3} \]

Then

\[ |A_iA_i| \le n_i^{2-\delta_\times}. \tag{10.4} \]

11. Final choice of \(\delta\)

Set

\[ \delta := \frac12\min(\delta_+,\delta_\times)>0. \tag{11.1} \]

Then for all sufficiently large \(i\),

\[ |A_i+A_i|\le |A_i|^{2-\delta} \]

and

\[ |A_iA_i|\le |A_i|^{2-\delta}. \]

Thus

\[ \max(|A_i+A_i|,|A_iA_i|) \le |A_i|^{2-\delta}. \]

Since \(|A_i|\to\infty\), discarding finitely many initial terms and relabeling gives an infinite sequence of finite subsets of \(\mathbb R\) satisfying the theorem.

\(\square\)

where:

  • \(C>\frac{\log2}{p}\) is chosen large;

the sumset satisfies

The earlier proof’s main unproved point was the lattice-counting assertion: one needs upper and lower estimates for lattice points of \(\sigma(\mathcal O_K)\) inside high-dimensional \(\ell^p\)-quasi-balls. The fix is to use the codifferent lattice and a standard transference bound to control the covering radius of \(\sigma(\mathcal O_K)\). This gives volume-counting estimates strong enough for the construction.

We use two standard external inputs:

  1. Martinet’s theorem: there are totally real number fields of arbitrarily large degree and bounded root discriminant.

  2. The Euclidean transference bound

    \[ \rho_2(\Lambda)\lambda_1(\Lambda^*)\le \frac d2 \]

    for a full-rank lattice \(\Lambda\subset \mathbb R^d\).

Everything else is proved below.

Proof

Let \(K/\mathbb Q\) be totally real of degree \(d\), with embeddings

\[ \sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_d:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb R. \]

Write

\[ \sigma(\alpha)=(\sigma_1(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_d(\alpha)). \]

For \(s>0\), define

\[ B_{s,d}(R) = \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^d:\sum_{j=1}^d |x_j|^s\le R^s \right\}. \]

Let

\[ V_{s,d}:=\operatorname{vol}(B_{s,d}(1)). \]

We use the formula

\[ V_{s,d} = \frac{\left(2\Gamma(1+1/s)\right)^d}{\Gamma(1+d/s)}. \tag{1} \]

This follows by evaluating

\[ \int_{\mathbb R^d} e^{-\sum |x_j|^s}\,dx \]

both as a product of one-dimensional integrals and by radial integration.

1. Volume saving under multiplication

For \(0<p\le 1\), define

\[ M_{p,d}:= \left(\frac{V_{p,d}^2}{V_{p/2,d}}\right)^{1/d}. \]

Using (1) and Stirling’s formula, for fixed \(p\),

\[ M_{p,d}\to m_p \]

as \(d\to\infty\), where

\[ m_p = 2^{1+2/p} \frac{\Gamma(1+1/p)^2}{\Gamma(1+2/p)}. \tag{2} \]

Moreover,

\[ m_p\sim 2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}} \qquad(p\to0). \tag{3} \]

Hence \(m_p\to\infty\) as \(p\to0\).

2. Additive and multiplicative containment

For \(0<p\le1\),

\[ |u+v|^p\le |u|^p+|v|^p. \]

Thus

\[ B_{p,d}(R)+B_{p,d}(R) \subseteq B_{p,d}(2^{1/p}R). \tag{4} \]

Also, if \(x,y\in B_{p,d}(R)\), then by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \sum_{j=1}^d |x_jy_j|^{p/2} \le \left(\sum |x_j|^p\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum |y_j|^p\right)^{1/2} \le R^p. \]

Since \((R^2)^{p/2}=R^p\), this gives

\[ B_{p,d}(R)\odot B_{p,d}(R) \subseteq B_{p/2,d}(R^2), \tag{5} \]

where \(\odot\) denotes coordinatewise multiplication.

3. Counting algebraic integers in \(B_{s,d}(R)\)

Let

\[ \Lambda_K:=\sigma(\mathcal O_K)\subset\mathbb R^d. \]

Then

\[ \det(\Lambda_K)=|D_K|^{1/2}=:\Delta_K. \]

Assume \(K\) has root discriminant bounded by \(B\), i.e.

\[ |D_K|^{1/d}\le B. \]

We prove the following counting estimate.

Lemma

For \(0<s\le1\), set

\[ \mu_s:=\frac B2 d^{1/s}. \]

Then for every \(R>\mu_s\),

\[ \frac{V_{s,d}R^d}{\Delta_K} \left(1-\left(\frac{\mu_s}{R}\right)^s\right)^{d/s} \le |\mathcal O_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)| \le \frac{V_{s,d}R^d}{\Delta_K} \left(1+\left(\frac{\mu_s}{R}\right)^s\right)^{d/s}. \tag{6} \]

Proof

The Euclidean dual lattice of \(\Lambda_K\) is

\[ \Lambda_K^*=\sigma(\mathfrak d_K^{-1}), \]

where \(\mathfrak d_K^{-1}\) is the codifferent.

If \(0\neq \beta\in\mathfrak d_K^{-1}\), then \(\beta\mathfrak d_K\subseteq \mathcal O_K\), so \((\beta)\mathfrak d_K\) is an integral ideal. Hence

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)|\,|D_K|\ge1. \]

Therefore

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)|\ge |D_K|^{-1}. \]

By AM–GM,

\[ \|\sigma(\beta)\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^d |\sigma_j(\beta)|^2 \ge d\left(\prod_{j=1}^d|\sigma_j(\beta)|^2\right)^{1/d} = d\,|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)|^{2/d}. \]

Thus

\[ \|\sigma(\beta)\|_2 \ge \sqrt d\,|D_K|^{-1/d} \ge \frac{\sqrt d}{B}. \]

So

\[ \lambda_1(\Lambda_K^*)\ge \frac{\sqrt d}{B}. \]

By the transference inequality,

\[ \rho_2(\Lambda_K)\lambda_1(\Lambda_K^*)\le \frac d2, \]

hence

\[ \rho_2(\Lambda_K)\le \frac B2\sqrt d. \]

For \(0<s\le1\),

\[ \|x\|_s \le d^{1/s-1/2}\|x\|_2. \]

Therefore the \(s\)-quasi-norm covering radius is at most

\[ \mu_s=\frac B2 d^{1/s}. \]

Choose a measurable fundamental domain \(F\) for \(\Lambda_K\) contained in \(B_{s,d}(\mu_s)\).

For the upper bound, if \(\lambda\in \Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)\) and \(f\in F\), then

\[ \lambda+f\in B_{s,d}\bigl((R^s+\mu_s^s)^{1/s}\bigr). \]

Thus

\[ |\Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)|\Delta_K \le V_{s,d}R^d \left(1+\left(\frac{\mu_s}{R}\right)^s\right)^{d/s}. \]

For the lower bound, let

\[ R_-=(R^s-\mu_s^s)^{1/s}. \]

Every point of \(B_{s,d}(R_-)\) lies in some translate \(\lambda+F\). If \(z=\lambda+f\) with \(z\in B_{s,d}(R_-)\) and \(f\in F\), then

\[ \|\lambda\|_s^s = \|z-f\|_s^s \le \|z\|_s^s+\|f\|_s^s \le R_-^s+\mu_s^s = R^s. \]

Hence \(\lambda\in\Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)\), so

\[ V_{s,d}R_-^d \le |\Lambda_K\cap B_{s,d}(R)|\Delta_K. \]

This is the lower bound in (6). \(\square\)

4. Choosing the number fields

By Martinet’s theorem, there exists an infinite sequence of totally real fields \(K_i\) with degrees

\[ d_i=[K_i:\mathbb Q]\to\infty \]

and uniformly bounded root discriminant

\[ |D_{K_i}|^{1/d_i}\le B. \]

Set

\[ \Delta_i:=|D_{K_i}|^{1/2}. \]

Then

\[ \Delta_i^{1/d_i}\le B^{1/2}. \tag{7} \]

Choose \(0<p\le1\) so small that

\[ m_p>B^{1/2}. \]

Choose \(\theta>0\) such that

\[ e^\theta B^{1/2}<m_p. \]

Since \(M_{p,d}\to m_p\), after discarding finitely many \(i\), we have

\[ \frac{M_{p,d_i}}{\Delta_i^{1/d_i}}\ge e^\theta. \tag{8} \]

Let

\[ q:=p/2, \qquad L:=\frac{\log 2}{p}. \]

5. Choosing the radii

For a large constant \(C>L\), define \(R_i\) by

\[ \frac{V_{p,d_i}R_i^{d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{Cd_i}. \tag{9} \]

Equivalently,

\[ R_i=e^C\Delta_i^{1/d_i}V_{p,d_i}^{-1/d_i}. \]

For fixed \(p\), Stirling’s formula implies

\[ \sup_d d^{1/p}V_{p,d}^{1/d}<\infty. \]

Therefore, for some constant \(H_p\),

\[ d^{1/p}V_{p,d}^{1/d}\le H_p \qquad\text{for all }d. \tag{10} \]

From (9) and \(\Delta_i^{1/d_i}\ge1\),

\[ \left(\frac{\mu_p}{R_i}\right)^p \le \left(\frac B2 H_p e^{-C}\right)^p =:\alpha(C), \]

and

\[ \left(\frac{\mu_q}{R_i^2}\right)^q \le \left(\frac B2 H_p^2 e^{-2C}\right)^q =:\beta(C). \]

Both \(\alpha(C)\) and \(\beta(C)\) tend to \(0\) as \(C\to\infty\). Choose \(C\) so large that \(\alpha(C),\beta(C)<1/2\), and with

\[ \eta(C):=\max\left(\frac{2\alpha(C)}p,\frac{\beta(C)}q\right), \]

we have

\[ \eta(C)<\frac{C-L}{4} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \eta(C)<\frac{\theta}{4}. \tag{11} \]

For this fixed \(C\), the counting lemma gives

\[ e^{(C-\eta)d_i} \le |\mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{p,d_i}(R_i)| \le e^{(C+\eta)d_i}, \tag{12} \]

where \(\eta=\eta(C)\).

6. Defining the sets \(A_i\)

Let

\[ P_i:=\mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{p,d_i}(R_i). \]

Choose one real embedding \(\sigma_{i,1}:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb R\), and define

\[ A_i:=\sigma_{i,1}(P_i)\subset\mathbb R. \]

Since \(\sigma_{i,1}\) is injective,

\[ |A_i|=|P_i|. \]

Write

\[ n_i:=|A_i|. \]

By (12),

\[ n_i\ge e^{(C-\eta)d_i}. \tag{13} \]

In particular \(n_i\to\infty\).

7. Sumset estimate

By (4),

\[ P_i+P_i \subseteq \mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{p,d_i}(2^{1/p}R_i). \]

Hence

\[ |A_i+A_i| = |P_i+P_i| \le |\mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{p,d_i}(2^{1/p}R_i)|. \]

Using the upper bound from the counting lemma and (9),

\[ |A_i+A_i| \le e^{\eta d_i} \frac{V_{p,d_i}(2^{1/p}R_i)^{d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{\eta d_i}2^{d_i/p}e^{Cd_i}. \]

Thus

\[ |A_i+A_i| \le e^{(C+L+\eta)d_i}. \tag{14} \]

Using (13),

\[ d_i\le \frac{\log n_i}{C-\eta}. \]

Therefore

\[ |A_i+A_i| \le n_i^{(C+L+\eta)/(C-\eta)}. \]

Now

\[ 2-\frac{C+L+\eta}{C-\eta} = \frac{C-L-3\eta}{C-\eta}>0 \]

by (11). Define

\[ \delta_+ := \frac{C-L-3\eta}{C-\eta}. \tag{15} \]

Then

\[ |A_i+A_i|\le n_i^{2-\delta_+}. \tag{16} \]

8. Product-set estimate

By (5),

\[ P_iP_i \subseteq \mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{q,d_i}(R_i^2), \qquad q=p/2. \]

Thus

\[ |A_iA_i| = |P_iP_i| \le |\mathcal O_{K_i}\cap B_{q,d_i}(R_i^2)|. \]

Using the counting lemma,

\[ |A_iA_i| \le e^{\eta d_i} \frac{V_{q,d_i}R_i^{2d_i}}{\Delta_i}. \tag{17} \]

From (9),

\[ R_i^{d_i} = \frac{e^{Cd_i}\Delta_i}{V_{p,d_i}}. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{V_{q,d_i}R_i^{2d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{2Cd_i} \Delta_i \frac{V_{q,d_i}}{V_{p,d_i}^2}. \]

Since

\[ M_{p,d_i}^{d_i} = \frac{V_{p,d_i}^2}{V_{q,d_i}}, \]

we get

\[ \frac{V_{q,d_i}R_i^{2d_i}}{\Delta_i} = e^{2Cd_i} \left(\frac{\Delta_i^{1/d_i}}{M_{p,d_i}}\right)^{d_i}. \]

By (8),

\[ \left(\frac{\Delta_i^{1/d_i}}{M_{p,d_i}}\right)^{d_i} \le e^{-\theta d_i}. \]

So (17) yields

\[ |A_iA_i| \le e^{(2C-\theta+\eta)d_i}. \tag{18} \]

Again using (13),

\[ |A_iA_i| \le n_i^{(2C-\theta+\eta)/(C-\eta)}. \]

Now

\[ 2-\frac{2C-\theta+\eta}{C-\eta} = \frac{\theta-3\eta}{C-\eta}>0 \]

by (11). Define

\[ \delta_\times := \frac{\theta-3\eta}{C-\eta}. \tag{19} \]

Then

\[ |A_iA_i| \le n_i^{2-\delta_\times}. \tag{20} \]

9. Final choice of \(\delta\)

Set

\[ \delta:=\frac12\min(\delta_+,\delta_\times)>0. \]

Then for all sufficiently large \(i\),

\[ |A_i+A_i|\le |A_i|^{2-\delta} \]

and

\[ |A_iA_i|\le |A_i|^{2-\delta}. \]

Thus

\[ \max(|A_i+A_i|,|A_iA_i|) \le |A_i|^{2-\delta}. \]

Since \(|A_i|\to\infty\), after discarding finitely many initial terms and relabeling, we obtain the required infinite sequence.

\(\square\)
